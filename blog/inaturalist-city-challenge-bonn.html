Sidewalk Forests and Secret Gardens: Finding Wild Nature in Bonn's Urban Cracks

April 2026  ·  Cory Whitney

I have been taking part in the iNaturalist City Nature Challenge over the past few days, and it has been nothing short of eye-opening. Armed with a phone and a new sense of curiosity, I have been wandering the streets of Bonn — and discovering that the most fascinating ecosystems are the ones hiding right beneath your feet.

The Miniature Forests of the Sidewalk

It started almost by accident. I crouched down to photograph a crack in the pavement near my front door and found myself staring into a tiny world: mosses, lichens, a seedling of something I did not yet know, and a beetle moving through it all like a dinosaur through jungle undergrowth. That crack was a forest in miniature — a bonsai ecosystem, complete and self-contained.

Over the following days I found the same story repeated across the city. Every gap between paving stones, every neglected strip at the base of a wall, every front garden with a small wild corner — all of them teemed with life. Bonn's urban fabric, when you look closely enough, is threaded through with nature.

"The most fascinating ecosystems are the ones hiding right beneath your feet — miniature bonsai forests growing in the cracks of city sidewalks."

What I Have Been Finding

The diversity has been genuinely surprising. In the space of a few city blocks I have recorded species across multiple kingdoms of life, including:

  • Wall-rue (Asplenium ruta-muraria)
  • Biting stonecrop (Sedum acre)
  • Pellitory-of-the-wall (Parietaria judaica)
  • Common liverwort (Marchantia polymorpha)
  • Various cushion mosses
  • Shepherd's purse (Capsella bursa-pastoris)
  • Annual meadow-grass (Poa annua)
  • Ground ivy (Glechoma hederacea)
  • Springtails and mites
  • Ground beetles and rove beetles
  • Solitary bee foragers
  • Lichens on old stone walls

Each observation sends me down a rabbit hole: looking up the life history of a plant, learning which insects depend on it, understanding why a particular species thrives in dry, nutrient-poor pavement gaps. The iNaturalist community has been brilliant for identifications — within hours of posting a photo I often have a confirmed species name and sometimes a helpful note about its ecology.

Tiny Front Gardens as Wild Refuges

Some of the richest spots I have found are the small front gardens along older streets in Bonn. Where a garden has been left a little wild — where the grass is allowed to grow long at the edges, where a patch of bare earth persists, where ivy climbs an old wall — the biodiversity shoots up. These spaces are tiny in area but outsized in ecological value.

It has made me think differently about urban "tidiness." A crack full of moss is not a maintenance failure; it is a habitat. A clump of weeds in a pavement gap is not a nuisance; it is a corridor connecting small green islands across the grey sea of the city.

Why the City Nature Challenge Matters

The iNaturalist City Nature Challenge is a global bioblitz — cities around the world compete (in the friendliest possible sense) to log as many observations of wild species as they can over a few days. The data feeds directly into real biodiversity science. Researchers use it to track species distributions, monitor urban ecology, and understand how nature persists in human-dominated landscapes.

But beyond the science, the challenge does something perhaps more immediately important: it teaches people to look. Once you have spent a morning hunting for life in sidewalk cracks, you cannot walk down a street the same way again. The city becomes layered — the built world on top, and just below it, a wild world that never stopped existing.

"Once you have spent a morning hunting for life in sidewalk cracks, you cannot walk down a street the same way again."

Join In

If you are in Bonn — or any city — I cannot recommend the experience enough. Download iNaturalist, pick a crack in a pavement, and look. You will be amazed at what is there. And your observations contribute to something real: a growing, open, global record of the biodiversity that shares our cities with us.
